FAILURE MODE PROCEDURES AND ROOT CAUSE ANALYSIS
================================================
Document Type: Failure Investigation Reference
Applicable Equipment: Industrial Manufacturing Machinery
Version: 1.0

---

OVERVIEW OF FAILURE MODES
--------------------------

This document describes the five primary failure modes observed in industrial manufacturing machines:

1. TWF — Tool Wear Failure
2. HDF — Heat Dissipation Failure
3. PWF — Power Failure
4. OSF — Overstrain Failure
5. RNF — Random Failure

Each failure mode has specific sensor signatures, root causes, and corrective actions.

---

FAILURE MODE 1: TOOL WEAR FAILURE (TWF)
----------------------------------------

Definition:
Tool Wear Failure (TWF) occurs when a cutting or forming tool degrades beyond its functional limit due to accumulated wear. The tool can no longer maintain the required precision or cutting force, leading to machine failure or poor product quality.

Typical sensor profile at TWF:
- Tool wear [min]: typically > 200 min at time of failure
- Torque [Nm]: progressively increasing as tool wears
- Process temperature [K]: elevated due to increased friction
- Rotational speed [rpm]: may decrease slightly due to increased resistance

Root causes of TWF:
- Exceeding recommended tool change intervals
- Incorrect tool material for the workpiece
- Insufficient cutting fluid / lubrication
- Excessive feed rate or depth of cut
- Vibration causing uneven wear

Corrective actions for TWF:
1. Stop the machine immediately upon detection
2. Remove and inspect the worn tool
3. Replace with a new tool of the correct specification
4. Review and recalibrate cutting parameters (speed, feed, depth)
5. Inspect workpiece for dimensional errors caused by the worn tool
6. Review tool change schedule and reduce interval if recurring

Preventive measures:
- Track cumulative tool wear time using the tool wear counter
- Set automated alerts at 80% of maximum recommended wear time
- Use the correct tool grade and coating for the material being processed
- Maintain consistent coolant supply to the cutting zone

FAILURE MODE 2: HEAT DISSIPATION FAILURE (HDF)
------------------------------------------------

Definition:
Heat Dissipation Failure (HDF) occurs when the machine's cooling system cannot remove heat fast enough, causing components to overheat. This is triggered when the difference between process temperature and air temperature drops below approximately 8.6 K AND rotational speed is below approximately 1380 rpm.

Typical sensor profile at HDF:
- Low rotational speed: < 1380 rpm
- Small temperature differential: Process temp – Air temp < 8.6 K
- Process temperature: may remain moderate but cooling is insufficient for current load
- Torque: may be normal or elevated

Physical explanation:
At low rotational speeds, less airflow is generated by the rotating components, reducing the convective cooling effect. If air temperature is also high, the machine cannot dissipate heat effectively. This is sometimes called thermal runaway if left unaddressed.

Root causes of HDF:
- Operating at very low speeds for extended periods
- High ambient temperature in the facility
- Blocked cooling channels or heat exchangers
- Coolant pump failure or low coolant level
- Fan motor failure
- Buildup of dust and debris on heat exchange surfaces

Corrective actions for HDF:
1. Reduce machine load or stop operation
2. Inspect cooling fans — verify they are running and unobstructed
3. Check coolant level, flow rate, and concentration
4. Clean heat exchanger fins and air filters
5. Verify ambient temperature is within specification
6. Inspect coolant pump for proper operation
7. Allow machine to cool before restarting

Preventive measures:
- Monitor process temperature – air temperature differential continuously
- Set alarm if differential exceeds or drops outside normal band (8–12 K)
- Maintain cooling system according to scheduled maintenance
- Ensure adequate airflow and ventilation around equipment

FAILURE MODE 3: POWER FAILURE (PWF)
-------------------------------------

Definition:
Power Failure (PWF) occurs when the actual power consumption of the machine falls outside the design operating window. This can happen if torque and rotational speed produce either too little or too much power.

Power (approximate) = Torque × Rotational Speed × (2π / 60) [Watts]

Typical sensor profiles that trigger PWF:
- High torque at very low speed: power < 3500 W (lower bound violation)
- High torque at high speed: power > 9000 W (upper bound violation)

Root causes of PWF:
- Motor overload: torque demand exceeds motor capacity
- Drive controller fault: speed setpoint error
- Mechanical binding: worn bearings or seized components
- Electrical supply issues: voltage fluctuation or phase imbalance
- Incorrect process parameters: wrong speed/feed combination
- Spindle damage

Corrective actions for PWF:
1. Stop the machine immediately
2. Check motor drive panel for fault codes
3. Inspect motor current with a clamp meter
4. Verify electrical supply voltage and phase balance
5. Inspect mechanical drive train for binding or damage
6. Review and correct the speed/torque operating setpoint
7. Contact electrical maintenance if voltage problems are found

Preventive measures:
- Monitor the torque × speed product (power proxy) continuously
- Set operating limits in the drive controller
- Inspect motor and drive components at scheduled intervals
- Verify electrical power quality periodically

FAILURE MODE 4: OVERSTRAIN FAILURE (OSF)
------------------------------------------

Definition:
Overstrain Failure (OSF) occurs when the mechanical force (torque) applied to the machine exceeds the structural strength of the tool or mechanical components, given the accumulated tool wear. The risk of OSF increases as tool wear accumulates because a worn tool requires more force to achieve the same material removal.

Overstrain threshold (approximate):
OSF risk = (Tool wear [min] × Torque [Nm]) > threshold constant

As tool wear increases, even moderate torque values can trigger OSF. This is why the relationship between torque AND tool wear must be monitored together.

Typical sensor profile at OSF:
- High torque [Nm]: significantly above normal for the material being processed
- High tool wear [min]: accumulated wear reduces tool strength
- Process temperature: elevated due to high mechanical stress
- Rotational speed: may be reduced due to overload

Root causes of OSF:
- Severely worn tool combined with continued full-load operation
- Incorrect machining parameters for current tool condition
- Unexpected hardness increase in the workpiece material
- Chatter or vibration causing instantaneous torque spikes
- Inadequate support or clamping of the workpiece

Corrective actions for OSF:
1. Stop the machine immediately
2. Inspect tool for fracture or severe wear
3. Replace tool before resuming operation
4. Reduce cutting parameters (feed rate, depth of cut) after tool change
5. Inspect mechanical components for damage from the overstrain event
6. Review workpiece material certification for unexpected hardness

Preventive measures:
- Monitor the combined torque × tool wear product
- Change tools proactively before reaching high wear levels
- Implement torque monitoring alarms
- Calibrate cutting parameters for each specific workpiece material

FAILURE MODE 5: RANDOM FAILURE (RNF)
--------------------------------------

Definition:
Random Failure (RNF) is a failure event that cannot be attributed to any specific measurable sensor pattern. It represents inherent random component failures — unexpected fractures, spontaneous electronic faults, or material defects — that statistical models cannot reliably predict from available sensor data.

Characteristics:
- No consistent sensor signature preceding failure
- Very low frequency in the dataset (approximately 0.1% of operations)
- Not reliably predictable by machine learning models

Root causes of RNF:
- Latent manufacturing defects in components
- Random electrical transients
- Undetected internal corrosion
- Spontaneous material fatigue at micro-structural level

Corrective actions for RNF:
1. Perform a full visual and functional inspection of all components
2. Check all electrical connections for loose terminals
3. Inspect for internal corrosion, cracking, or material defects
4. Review recent process history for any unusual events
5. Replace any component showing signs of abnormality

Preventive measures:
- Strict incoming quality inspection of replacement parts
- Regular electrical insulation testing
- Periodic non-destructive testing (NDT) of high-stress components
- Maintain detailed failure history log for pattern analysis

FAILURE INVESTIGATION PROCEDURE
---------------------------------

When any failure event is detected, follow this structured investigation process:

Step 1: Isolate and Secure
- Stop the machine immediately
- Apply lockout/tagout (LOTO) procedure
- Prevent access by unauthorised personnel

Step 2: Record Evidence
- Note all sensor readings at time of failure
- Photograph the machine, tool, and workpiece
- Record the last known good operating parameters

Step 3: Identify Failure Mode
- Review sensor data to match against TWF, HDF, PWF, OSF, or RNF profiles
- Check tool wear counter
- Review recent temperature and torque trends

Step 4: Root Cause Analysis
- Use the 5-Why method to trace root cause
- Review maintenance history
- Inspect related components that may have contributed

Step 5: Corrective Action
- Apply the specific corrective action for the identified failure mode
- Verify machine operation before returning to production

Step 6: Preventive Action
- Update maintenance schedule based on findings
- Adjust monitoring thresholds if necessary
- Brief the operations team on lessons learned
